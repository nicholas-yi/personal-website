    Episode-Grounded Lifecycle Reconciliation for Conversational Agent Memory. Co-author; arXiv preprint.

    preprint Sep 2026/ co-author/ arXiv ↗/ PDF ↗
    When a conversation changes Notes from Stashbird, on what a memory keeps and what it lets go.

    I want an agent to remember what I said. I also want to be allowed to stop meaning it. A plan is still a plan when I change the time; a preference can be sincere without being permanent. Neither feels like an unusual thing to ask of a conversation.

    It becomes less straightforward when the conversation has already been turned into something else. A message becomes an entity, a relation, a searchable passage. It might also lend a few words to a summary that no longer makes its source obvious. The original sentence is now doing work in places I cannot see.

    Removing the message is only the beginning. What matters is whether its consequences still answer for me.

    That is the distinction I find interesting in Stashbird. Source episodes remain connected to the entities and relations derived from them. When the source changes, those connections give the memory a way to revise what it holds, rather than simply placing a newer sentence beside an older one and hoping retrieval chooses well.

    What still has a reason to stay?

    Suppose two episodes support the same relation. Deleting one should withdraw its contribution, but it should not pretend that the other episode never happened. In Stashbird, repeated evidence extends a relation's support. A correction supersedes the active relation while retaining its earlier version as history. When the last supporting episode is removed, the unsupported relation leaves both the graph and the search index.

    There is an awkward case underneath that tidy description. An entity can lose the evidence behind its metadata and still connect surviving neighbors. Removing it entirely could disturb information that still has support. The published method can retain the connective identity while clearing the unsupported metadata; affected community summaries leave retrieval until they are regenerated.

    So the question is not simply whether something is present in the graph. It is what that remaining structure is allowed to tell us. Keeping a connection is not permission to keep every claim once attached to it.

    What the test can tell us

    The paper separates question answering from controlled updates and deletions. In the lifecycle studies, semantic decisions are held fixed, so the experiment can isolate whether reconciliation reaches the intended state. That is useful evidence about the mechanism. It is not, on its own, evidence that every model-generated interpretation of a conversation will be right.

    I co-authored Stashbird. My related work in Microsoft Teams included co-engineering provenance-aware memory and owning evaluation across memory systems and benchmarks. Isolated runs, stage-level telemetry, and recovery helped distinguish infrastructure failures from model-quality regressions. This is a team project; the method described here comes from our co-authored paper.

    What I'd test next

    I'd put a revised memory in the path of a decision: change an earlier preference or remove one source, then ask whether the agent takes the right action (and whether it knows when to check). I'd keep the task, tools, and model fixed, and compare against no memory and an authoritative current-state reference. Recalling the new fact is one check. Acting on it appropriately is another. This is a proposed experiment, not a result from the Stashbird paper.

    Research that shapes the question

    CorpGen offers a useful lens on memory interference and changing dependencies across interleaved tasks. Its reuse of verified demonstrations also raises a question: what improved — the memory, the plan, the tools, or access to a successful example?

    Related reading: CorpGen, version 1 (§3.6 on demonstration reuse; §5.3 describes a preliminary artifact-versus-trace evaluation observation, separate from its main experiments).

    I want memory that leaves room for a conversation to change. But I do not think that means keeping less indiscriminately, any more than remembering well means keeping everything. It means being able to explain why a fact is still there, and to let it go when that explanation no longer holds.

Thinking out loud

What makes an evaluation trustworthy? On the distance between a reassuring number and a result worth trusting.

A better score is reassuring. I do not think that reassurance is necessarily misplaced; a comparison can tell us something real. I just want to know which comparison we actually made.

Was the answer wrong, or did the run never reach an answer? It is a small distinction until both attempts become the same cell in a table. Then the distinction is most of what I need to understand.
